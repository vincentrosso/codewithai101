# QA Track, Lesson 5 — Claude as a QA Thinking Partner
*v1.0.0*

**Goal for today:** use Claude to attack your own test suite — find the partitions you missed, the combinations you didn't enumerate, the risks you didn't name — and then measure how much of what it gave you was actually real. By end of day your suite is bigger, every added case has been *executed*, and you have a number for Claude's hit rate that you found yourself.

**Time budget:** ~85 min hands-on, ~25 min journal, ~10 min slack.

---

## 1. The rules for this lesson (~5 min)

You've done this dance twice before — Week 2 when Claude came online in the browser, Week 4 when Claude Code got access to the repo. Same shape, new domain:

1. **Claude cannot see the app.** It has never loaded the Toolshop. Everything it tells you about the Toolshop specifically is inference from a million other web stores. That inference is often good and it is *never evidence*.
2. **A generated test case is a hypothesis, not a test.** It counts when you've run it. Until then it's a sentence.
3. **You supply the facts, it supplies the coverage.** Field names, error text, prices, status codes: yours, observed. Its job is the combinatorics and the blind spots.
4. **Never paste a generated bug report to a developer unedited.** More on that in section 6, because it's the one that costs you your reputation rather than an hour.
5. **You judge every suggestion in writing.** That's the deliverable — not the suggestions.

Browser Claude (claude.ai) is fine today. Lesson 6 goes back to Claude Code.

## 2. Gap analysis on your own suite (~25 min)

Open a new conversation. Give it real context first — this is the difference between generic output and useful output:

> I'm testing the cart and checkout of an online tool store. I'll give you the app's
> actual behavior as I observed it, then my test suite. I want you to find gaps.
>
> Observed facts:
> - Cart quantity field: a number input with + / − steppers, accepts typed values
> - Checkout steps: cart → sign in → billing address → payment → confirm
> - Payment methods offered: <the real list you read off the screen>
> - Billing address fields: <the real ones>
> - Prices range from $3.55 to <the real max>
>
> Here is my suite:
> <paste cases/cart-checkout.md>
>
> Which equivalence partitions, boundary values, and condition combinations are
> missing? For each suggestion, tell me which technique it comes from, and mark
> any suggestion that depends on an assumption about this app that I haven't
> confirmed.

That last clause matters more than the rest of the prompt. Asking it to flag its own assumptions doesn't make it honest, but it does separate "you're missing the empty-cart checkout case" (structurally true of any store) from "test the promo code field" (a field that may not exist).

Then make a second pass with a different lens, because one prompt gets you one shape of answer:

> Now act as a developer who wrote this checkout badly. What did you take shortcuts
> on, and what would you be most afraid a tester would try?

That reframing — a **pre-mortem** — reliably produces different and better material than "list test cases." It's worth remembering as a general technique: asking for failure modes gets you sharper output than asking for coverage.

## 3. Judge everything, in writing (~20 min)

Do not paste its list into your suite. Open `cases/ai-gap-review.md` and put every suggestion in a table with a verdict:

| # | Suggestion | Verdict | Note |
|---|-----------|---------|------|
| 1 | Checkout with an empty cart | **REAL — new** | Genuine gap, added as CART-21 |
| 2 | Quantity field with `1.5` | DUPLICATE | Already CART-04 [EP] |
| 3 | Apply an expired promo code | **HALLUCINATED** | No promo code field exists in this app |
| 4 | Order over the stock level | REAL — new | Added CART-22, but stock isn't visible in UI → BLOCKED |
| 5 | SQL injection in the address field | OUT OF SCOPE | Security testing, plan §2 |

Five verdicts, and the discipline is to use them honestly:

- **REAL — new** — a genuine gap. Add it to the suite.
- **DUPLICATE** — you already had it. Common, and not a failure; it's weak confirmation your suite was decent.
- **HALLUCINATED** — describes a feature, field, or message that doesn't exist. This is the important column, and you can only fill it in because you spent Lesson 1 mapping the app.
- **VAGUE** — "test error handling." Not a case; can't fail; can't be executed.
- **OUT OF SCOPE** — real but not your job this cycle.

Then count. Write the tally at the top of the file:

```
28 suggestions: 7 real-new, 12 duplicate, 4 hallucinated, 3 vague, 2 out of scope.
Real-new rate: 25%.
```

That number is the point of today. Not "AI is good at testing" or "AI makes things up" — *your* measured rate on *your* suite, which is the only version of the question that helps you decide how much to lean on it next time. Expect the duplicate column to be the biggest one, and expect that to feel deflating. It shouldn't: a 25% real-new rate on a suite you already thought was finished is an excellent trade for twenty minutes.

Also look at *which technique* the real-new suggestions clustered in. If five of your seven gaps were boundary values, your BVA muscle is the weak one, and that's a more useful thing to learn about yourself than the hit rate.

## 4. Execute the new cases (~15 min)

Every case with verdict REAL — new goes into `cases/cart-checkout.md`, tagged `[AI]` alongside its technique tag, and then **you run it**. Log the result in your execution log like any other case.

Do this today, not "later." An unexecuted generated case is the most dangerous artifact in this track: it looks exactly like coverage in a file, it counts in nobody's tally as untested, and it has never once touched the software. A suite quietly padded with hypotheses is worse than a small suite, because you'll believe it.

If any of them fails, file the bug with the full Lesson 3 treatment. Note in the report that the case came from a gap analysis — not because the developer cares, but because *you* want to know at the end of the year which bugs your AI-assisted passes actually caught.

## 5. Combinatorics and other things it's genuinely good at (~10 min)

Gap analysis is the headline use. Three more that reliably pay, and they share a shape — **you own the facts, it owns the enumeration**:

**Decision tables and pairwise sets.** Give it your real conditions and their real values and ask for the full table, or for a pairwise-reduced set with the reasoning shown. This is mechanical work with an exact right answer, which is the safest possible thing to delegate. Spot-check three rows against your own reading of the app.

**Turning session notes into a report.** Paste your terse charter notes and ask it to structure them into the Lesson 3 bug-report format. It's good at the scaffolding. It is *not* allowed to supply content: read every line of what comes back and delete anything you didn't observe. Which brings us to the trap.

**Critiquing your written work.** Paste `plan/test-plan.md` and ask what a senior QA lead would push back on. Criticism of your reasoning is safer ground than generation of facts, because you can evaluate every point against a document you wrote.

## 6. The trap, stated plainly (~10 min)

Run this deliberately, because you need to see it once with your own eyes.

Give Claude a *deliberately thin* bug note — three words, no specifics: *"cart total wrong sometimes."* Ask it to write a complete bug report.

Read what comes back. Somewhere in it there will be a browser version you never stated, an exact price that appeared from nowhere, a step count, maybe a console error message with a plausible file name. Nothing in that report is marked as invented. It reads exactly like the report you wrote yourself on Lesson 3 — same confident structure, same specificity — and roughly half of it is furniture.

Now imagine filing it. A developer spends forty minutes failing to reproduce steps that were never performed, and closes it "cannot reproduce." The next real bug you file gets read with that memory attached. **The cost of a hallucinated bug report isn't the wasted hour; it's that your reports stop being trusted** — and being trusted is most of a tester's actual power in an organization.

The rule that follows is short: **facts come from your hands and your eyes; Claude gets structure, coverage, and criticism.** Every specific in a report you file — a version, a price, a status code, an error string — is something you personally observed.

Save the generated report to `bugs/hallucination-demo.md` with your annotations marking each invented detail. Keep it. It's the most useful artifact you'll produce today.

## 8. Journal (~25 min)

`journal/QA05.md`:

```markdown
# QA Lesson 5

## What I did
(short list)

## The numbers
Your tally, and your real-new rate. Which technique did the genuine gaps cluster in,
and what does that say about which of your own muscles is weakest?

## The best thing it found
Name the single most valuable gap it caught. Why did you miss it? Be specific — was
it a partition you didn't think of, or one you decided wasn't worth testing?

## The hallucination
Quote one invented detail from the thin-note bug report. What made it believable?
How would you have caught it if you hadn't already known it was fake?

## A line I accepted but still don't fully grasp
One suggestion you added to your suite while not being fully sure *why* it's a good
test. Name it. That's the next thing to chase.
```
