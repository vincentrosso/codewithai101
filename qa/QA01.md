# QA Track, Lesson 1 — What QA Actually Is, and the Test Plan
*v1.0.0*

**Goal for today:** meet the practice app you'll test for the rest of this track, learn where "expected result" actually comes from when nobody wrote a spec, and write a one-page test plan for one feature area. By the end you have a `qa-lab` repo with a real plan in it — not a document nobody reads, a page that decides what you will and won't spend your time on.

**Time budget:** ~75 min hands-on, ~20 min journal, ~25 min slack.

---

## About this track

You finished twelve weeks of building. You wrote code, tested it with `pytest`, put it behind CI, and shipped it. This track flips the chair around: instead of building the thing, you're the person who decides whether the thing is good enough to ship.

That job has a name — QA — and it is not "click around and see if anything looks broken." It's a discipline with its own techniques, its own vocabulary, and its own failure modes. Six lessons:

- **L1 (today):** what QA is, oracles, the test plan.
- **L2:** test case design — the techniques that get you 80% coverage with 20% of the cases.
- **L3:** exploratory testing and the bug report.
- **L4:** regression, risk, and where automation actually belongs.
- **L5:** Claude as a QA *thinking* partner — and the specific way it lies to you.
- **L6:** Claude Code as a QA *engineer* — automating the smoke suite and wiring it into CI.

Lessons 1–4 use no AI. Same rule as every foundations day in the main course, and for the same reason: you cannot judge Claude's test cases in Lesson 5 if you've never written your own.

### The practice app

Everything in this track targets one app: **the Toolshop** at [practicesoftwaretesting.com](https://practicesoftwaretesting.com) — an online hardware store built specifically as a testing playground. It has a product catalog, filters, search, a cart, checkout, user accounts, and an admin area. It also has an API at `api.practicesoftwaretesting.com`, which Lesson 6 will use.

Two seeded accounts (verify them yourself in section 2 — seeded data gets reset):

## 2. Map the app before you judge it (~15 min)

Open [practicesoftwaretesting.com](https://practicesoftwaretesting.com). Do **not** start hunting bugs. Today you're a cartographer, not a critic.

Click through the whole app once and write down what exists. Log in with the customer account. Look at the catalog, the filters down the left, the sort dropdown, the search box, a product page, the cart, the checkout steps, your account pages. Then log out and log in as admin and look at what's different.

Write `plan/app-map.md` — a plain list of every screen and, for each, what a user can *do* there:

```markdown
# Toolshop — app map (as of YYYY-MM-DD)

## Catalog (/)
- Search box (free text)
- Filters: category checkboxes, brand checkboxes, price slider
- Sort: name A-Z / Z-A, price low-high / high-low
- Product grid, paginated

## Product detail (/product/:id)
- Quantity stepper
- Add to cart
- Add to favourites (requires login?)
...
```

Two things to notice while you do this:

- **You are building the thing there is no document for.** Nobody handed you a spec. This map *is* your spec until someone contradicts it.
- **Write down what surprises you** — a control that seems to do nothing, a screen you can reach two different ways, a price that looks wrong. Don't investigate yet. A one-line note per surprise, at the bottom, under `## Questions`. Lesson 3 is where those get chased.

Verify the two logins work and note anything the admin sees that the customer doesn't.

## 3. QA is not testing (~10 min)

Three words get used interchangeably and shouldn't be:

- **Testing** — actively investigating a product to find out information about its quality. You run things and observe. It's an *information-gathering* activity.
- **Quality control (QC)** — checking the output against a standard. Did this build pass the checks? Gate open, gate closed.
- **Quality assurance (QA)** — the whole process that makes defects less likely in the first place: how requirements get written, how code gets reviewed, how releases get cut. Testing is one part of it.

Your title in most companies will say QA and your day will be mostly testing. Fine. But keep the distinction, because it changes what you do when you find a bug. A tester files the bug. Someone doing QA also asks *"why did nobody catch this until now, and what would have caught it earlier?"* — and that question is worth more than the bug.

Now the sentence that reframes the whole job:

> **You cannot prove software works. You can only sample it and report what you found.**

The number of possible inputs to the Toolshop's search box is effectively infinite. You will never test them all, and every hour you spend on search is an hour you're not spending on checkout. Testing is a *sampling* problem under a time budget. Everything in Lesson 2 (design techniques) and Lesson 4 (risk) exists to make that sample smarter than random.

So the honest deliverable of a testing effort is never "it works." It's: *here is what I tried, here is what I found, here is what I did not have time to look at.* That last clause is the professional one. Most testers skip it.

## 4. Where "expected result" comes from — oracles (~10 min)

You add a hammer to the cart and the cart badge shows `1`. Is that correct?

You said yes instantly, but *why*? Nobody wrote "the badge shall display the count of items." You used something to decide. That something has a name: an **oracle** — any principle you use to judge whether behavior is right.

The common oracles, roughly in order of how much authority they carry:

1. **Specification** — a written requirement, a ticket, a design mockup. Strongest, and usually absent or out of date.
2. **Consistency with itself** — the app does the same thing in two places, or the same thing it did yesterday. If sort-by-price works on the catalog but not in search results, one of them is wrong even if you can't say which.
3. **Consistency with comparable products** — every other online store on earth puts the cart in the top right and lets you remove an item from it. Deviating isn't automatically a bug, but it demands a reason.
4. **User expectation** — a reasonable person would be surprised or annoyed. Soft, arguable, and still the source of the most valuable bugs.
5. **Purpose** — the feature exists to accomplish something. A checkout that completes but never emails the customer technically "worked."
6. **Standards and law** — accessibility, currency formatting, privacy, tax rules.

Write these six down somewhere you'll see them. In Lesson 3 you'll file bug reports, and *every* report has to name its oracle — otherwise you're just asserting taste at a developer, and they'll bounce it back as "working as designed."

**Try it now.** Add a product to the cart, then set the quantity in the cart to `0`. Before you press anything: predict what happens. Write the prediction down. Then do it. Then write which oracle you used to decide whether what happened was OK. If your prediction was wrong, say so in the journal — that's the point of predicting.

## 5. Write the test plan (~25 min)

A test plan is not a 40-page template with a signature block. It's one page that answers: what are we testing, what are we *not* testing, what would make us stop, and what could go wrong.

Pick **one feature area** to own for this track. Recommended: **cart and checkout** — it's where the money is, it has multiple steps, and it touches login, stock, and pricing. (Search is the other decent choice. Don't pick "the whole app.")

Write `plan/test-plan.md`:

```markdown
# Test plan — Toolshop cart & checkout
*Author: Tyler · Date: YYYY-MM-DD · v1*

## 1. What I'm testing
The cart and checkout flow: adding items, changing quantities, removing items,
the multi-step checkout, and what an order looks like afterward in the account area.

## 2. What I'm NOT testing (and why)
- Admin area — different user, different risk profile, out of budget this cycle.
- Payment processing against a real gateway — the demo app doesn't have one.
- Performance / load — explicitly out of scope, and the app is a shared demo.
- Browsers other than Chrome — noted as a coverage gap, see section 6.

## 3. Environment
- App: https://practicesoftwaretesting.com (shared demo, data may reset)
- Browser: Chrome <version>, macOS <version>
- Accounts: seeded customer account; a second account I register myself

## 4. Approach
- Designed test cases for the flows I can enumerate (Lesson 2)
- Time-boxed exploratory sessions for the parts I can't (Lesson 3)
- A tagged smoke subset that I can re-run in ~10 minutes (Lesson 4)

## 5. Entry / exit criteria
- Entry: app reachable, both seeded logins work, cart empty.
- Exit: every P1 case executed; no open P1 bugs; every unexecuted case listed
  by name with the reason.

## 6. Known gaps
Anything I decided not to cover, listed honestly so nobody assumes it was covered.
```

Fill it in for real — your words, your scope decisions, not mine. The two sections that people skip are **2** and **6**, and they're the two that protect you. A test plan that lists only what you covered lets everyone else assume you covered everything else too.

## 6. Risk list (~10 min)

Add `plan/risks.md`. Five risks in your feature area, each scored:

| # | Risk | Likelihood (1-3) | Impact (1-3) | Score | Why |
|---|------|------------------|--------------|-------|-----|
| 1 | Cart total miscalculated with multiple quantities | 2 | 3 | 6 | Wrong charge = money and trust |
| 2 | Cart lost on logout/login | 2 | 2 | 4 | Common failure, users abandon |
| ... | | | | | |

Score = likelihood × impact. Sort descending. That order is the order you'll write test cases in tomorrow — not the order the menus happen to appear in.

The reason to write this down rather than hold it in your head: risk is where you'll be *challenged*. When someone asks "why didn't you test the admin area," a scored list is an answer. Intuition isn't.
